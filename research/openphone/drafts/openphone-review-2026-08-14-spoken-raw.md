Quo used to be called OpenPhone. The new name matters less than the reason people choose it. It turns a business number into a shared space for calls, texts, contacts, and notes.

That sounds basic until two people need to answer the same customer. With a normal mobile number, one person has the history and everybody else asks what happened. With Quo, the team can see the conversation, leave a private note, assign the next job, and keep using the same public number.

This is the part Quo gets right. The app is much easier to understand than an old office phone system. It feels closer to a modern chat app than a box of telephone settings.

The hard part is also simple. A business phone has to work every time a customer calls. A clean screen cannot make up for delayed audio, a missed message, or a number stuck during a move.

We recommend Quo for a small team that needs to share calls and texts without buying desk phones. Do not move your main number on day one. First run real calls, real messages, and a support test with a temporary number. The daily experience is strong enough to earn a trial. The failure cases are serious enough to make that trial mandatory.

## What Quo replaces

A small company often starts with a personal mobile number. It works until the owner hires someone, takes a holiday, or needs to know which customer is still waiting.

Quo lets the business use a separate number through its existing phones and computers. Calls and texts stay away from the owner's private number. Several people can work from one shared line.

Each customer gets one conversation history. A call, text, voicemail, recording, and internal note can sit in the same place. A teammate does not need to search email or ask for a summary before replying.

This is useful for a repair company, clinic, agency, property manager, or local service business. The customer keeps calling one number even when the person answering changes.

Quo also has call menus and routing. A call menu is the recorded choice that says, for example, press one for sales and two for support. Routing decides which person or group rings next.

The higher plans add call reports, AI summaries, transcripts, and more connections to sales tools. Quo also offers an AI receptionist called Sona. It can answer when the team is busy, collect details, and pass the conversation back.

None of that changes the main value. Quo gives a small team one clear place to handle a business number.

## The shared conversation is the best part

Most small companies do not need a complex call center. They need to answer, remember, and follow up.

Quo is built around that job. When a customer texts after a call, the message does not begin a separate record. It joins the same conversation. The next employee can see what was said and avoid asking the customer to repeat it.

Private comments help with handoffs. One person can tag a coworker and ask a question inside the customer record. The customer does not see that note.

Assignments make ownership clear. A shared inbox is only useful when everyone knows who should reply. Without assignments, a team can look at the same unanswered message and assume somebody else has it.

The product also works on a phone and a computer. That matters for a company whose staff move between a desk, a job site, and home. They can use the business number without carrying a second phone.

This is why Quo scores well for both setup and product. It removes the awkward parts of sharing a number without turning every employee into a phone-system expert.

## Call quality is not a small complaint

G2 has more than 3,300 Quo reviews and the overall rating is very high. Ease of use, setup, support, and call recording receive a lot of praise. The same G2 summary also records hundreds of mentions of connection trouble, plus dropped calls and delays.

The smaller community sample is rougher. Some businesses describe delayed audio, missing sound, dropped calls, and messages that reach the phone later than the computer. Other long-term users say the service has been dependable and remains better than the choices they tried.

We would not average those experiences into a vague middle. Call quality has to be judged on the exact phones, networks, places, and hours your business uses.

Make calls from the office, home, a car park, and the weakest mobile-data area where staff work. Use Wi-Fi and mobile data. Call a mobile number and a landline. Keep one call open for at least 20 minutes.

Listen for delay. Delay is the pause that makes both people speak at once. Also check for one-way audio, short gaps, a dropped call, and a call that never rings.

Run the same tests on every phone model the team uses. A perfect call on the owner's new iPhone does not prove that an older Android device will behave the same way.

The result needs to be boring. If the team notices the phone system during a normal call, something is wrong.

## Texting has rules outside Quo

Business texting in the United States and Canada involves carrier rules. A company may need to register its brand and explain the types of messages it plans to send. Carriers can block or limit traffic that does not meet their rules.

That process is not unique to Quo, but Quo is where the customer experiences it. A business that buys the product on Monday should not assume every text will be ready on Tuesday.

Test a normal reply, a photo, a link, and a message to a new contact. Check both directions. Ask the recipient when the message arrived and whether the number looked correct.

Do not use a virtual business number for bank codes or other one-time passwords without checking first. Some banks and online services refuse to send security codes to internet-based numbers. No phone provider can promise that every outside company will accept the number.

If texting drives sales or appointments, complete registration and delivery tests before printing the number on ads.

## Number porting deserves its own plan

Porting means moving an existing phone number from one provider to another. The number may appear simple, but ownership records, account names, addresses, and carrier rules sit behind it.

A failed port can leave the number working at the old provider, stuck between providers, or unavailable. That is why the move should be treated like a small business change, not a settings task.

Start with the exact account details held by the current carrier. Do not copy the business name from memory. Ask for the service address, account number, porting code, and the name on the account.

Keep the old service active until Quo confirms the move is complete and you have tested incoming calls and texts. Cancelling early can put the number at risk.

Choose a quiet period. Tell staff how customers will reach the business if the number stops working. Keep a second number ready for the website, email footer, and social pages.

Then test support before the port. Ask a real question about the records needed for your current carrier. The answer will tell you whether the team can reach a person and whether that person understands the move.

## Support is the reason the score stops at 7.9

Support is not simply good or bad here. Large review samples contain a lot of praise for fast, helpful replies. The difficult cases show another pattern.

Normal setup questions are often resolved well. Porting, account locks, service loss, and billing problems can take longer and pass between people. That difference matters because a business phone becomes urgent the moment it stops working.

A company with a live number does not only need a polite reply. It needs one person to own the problem, explain the next step, and stay with it until calls work again.

Test that before moving the main line. Ask support a question that the help center does not answer in one paragraph. Record how long the first useful reply takes. Check whether the same person keeps the case or whether you must repeat the story.

Also read the current support terms for your plan. A team that requires phone support, a fixed response time, or an account manager should confirm those points in writing. Do not assume a company selling phones provides support by phone.

## Price and value

Starter costs $19 per person each month, or $15 with annual billing. It includes one number per person and the main calling, texting, voicemail, shared-number, and collaboration tools.

Business costs $33 per person monthly, or $23 annually. It adds call forwarding, call reports, AI summaries and transcripts, and more advanced connections.

Scale costs $47 per person monthly, or $35 annually. It adds deeper AI tagging and controls for larger teams.

Extra numbers cost $5 a month. Taxes, international calls, messaging registration, and other usage can add to the total.

The price is reasonable when several people need to share customer conversations. It is less attractive for one person who only wants a second number and does not need notes, assignments, recordings, or team history.

Build the quote around the real team. Count every person who needs access, every public number, and the plan needed for routing or integrations. Add taxes and expected calling costs. Compare the yearly total, not the price shown beside one seat.

Start monthly unless the annual saving clearly pays for the risk. A live phone system is hard to replace quickly, so a long contract should follow a successful trial.

## Who should use Quo?

Quo is best for a small company where two or more people answer the same customers. It works well when calls and texts need to stay together and the team wants context without buying a full call-center system.

It is also useful for a solo owner who wants a clear line between work and private life. The value is strongest if the owner texts customers, saves call history, or expects to add an assistant.

Look elsewhere if the company needs live call coaching, complex call-center reporting, firm support response times, or proven service across many countries. A larger phone platform may be less pleasant to use but stronger in those areas.

We would also skip Quo if the trial shows unstable calls at a location the business cannot avoid. The interface is not a reason to tolerate bad audio.

## Verdict

Quo is a very good shared business phone app. It makes calls and texts easy to hand between people, keeps customer history clear, and avoids much of the language and clutter found in older phone systems.

That is the recommendation. The warning is that the phone still has to behave like a phone.

Use a temporary number for the trial. Test every device and network the team relies on. Confirm message registration. Call support with a real question. Prepare the port and the backup route before touching the main number.

If those tests pass, Quo is one of the easiest business phone systems for a small team to live with. If call quality or support breaks during the trial, believe the trial and choose something else.
