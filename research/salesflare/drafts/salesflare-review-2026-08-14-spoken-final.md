Salesflare is a CRM for small companies that sell to other businesses. A CRM is the shared place where the team keeps contacts, emails, meetings, notes, possible deals, and the next job to do.

Its main promise is less typing. Salesflare connects to the tools the team already uses and builds much of the customer record automatically.

That is useful because most CRM problems do not begin with a missing feature. They begin when somebody has a good call, promises to follow up, and never updates the system.

We recommend Salesflare for a small business-to-business sales team that wants the system to fill in more of the record for them.

We would test Salesflare by following one real lead. Start with an email, book a meeting, send a proposal, hand the account to a colleague, and let the lead go quiet. The record should show what happened, who owns the next step, and when somebody needs to act.

## Automatic data entry is the reason to consider it

Salesflare can pull contact details, emails, calendar meetings, and other activity into one timeline. Instead of typing every interaction into a form, the salesperson can open the company and see the history.

This can remove a lot of dull work. It can also make the record look complete when it is not.

Import 100 contacts that reflect the real customer list. Include two people with the same name and several people from one company. Add old addresses, aliases, forwarded messages, personal email accounts, and a contact who changed jobs.

Then compare Salesflare with the inbox and calendar. Did it attach the right meeting to the right company? Did it merge two people who should stay separate? Did it create duplicates? Can an ordinary user correct the mistake without asking an administrator?

Automatic does not mean correct. The time saving is real only when the team spends less time fixing the record than it used to spend entering it.

## The timeline should make a handoff easy

A customer timeline is simply the ordered history of calls, messages, meetings, notes, and changes around that account.

Give one active lead to a second salesperson without a private briefing. Ask that person three questions: What has the lead asked for? What have we promised? What should happen next?

The answers should be obvious from the record. Private chatter should not drown out customer facts. An automated email should look different from a personal reply. The latest note should not hide an older promise.

This is where a shared CRM proves its value. The second person should not need to search Slack, ask a colleague, and read a whole email chain. If they do, the company has moved the mess instead of fixing it.

## A pipeline is only a useful picture when the stages mean something

The pipeline is the board where possible deals move through steps such as new lead, meeting booked, proposal sent, and won or lost.

Those labels sound simple. Teams often use them differently. One person may move a deal to proposal when a document is drafted. Another may wait until the customer has opened it.

Define each stage before importing everything. Every stage needs an entry rule, an exit rule, an owner, and a normal next action.

Now test a deal that does not follow the neat route. Let it pause, reopen, split into two opportunities, or involve two companies. Check whether Salesflare can represent the situation without false stages and confusing workarounds.

Salesflare's focused design is likely to suit a short, ordinary B2B sales process. The same simplicity can become restrictive for a company with several products, approval layers, territories, or unusual reporting rules.

## Follow-up reminders matter more than a busy dashboard

A salesperson needs to know who deserves attention today. That is more useful than a screen full of activity numbers.

Create ten test deals. Give some a clear next task, leave others idle, and mark one as lost. Let a lead reply to an email in the middle of an automated follow-up series.

Salesflare should stop the wrong email, bring the reply to the owner, and move the next action into view. A reminder should disappear when the work is complete. A lost deal should not keep producing noise.

Pro includes multi-step email workflows. These are planned sequences of messages and follow-up steps. These messages can help a small team stay consistent. They still need to stop when a person replies, opts out, changes company, or asks not to be tracked.

Send the sequence only to internal test addresses first. Check timing, sender identity, replies, unsubscribe handling, duplicate sends, and what happens when ownership changes. Review the email rules and consent requirements that apply to the audience.

## Email tracking is useful in smaller doses

Salesflare can track email, link, and website activity. That may tell a salesperson when interest is fresh. It does not reveal what the customer thinks.

An open can be triggered by a security scanner. Images can be blocked. A link may be checked by a company system before a human sees it. Several opens might mean strong interest, or nothing at all.

Treat tracking as a weak signal. Do not write a different sales story around every notification.

The team should also decide when tracking is appropriate. Check privacy law, customer expectations, internal policy, and the effect on email delivery. Make sure a user can turn it off for a sensitive conversation.

## Reporting has to answer the weekly questions

Growth includes standard dashboards. Pro adds custom dashboards. A dashboard is simply a saved view of the numbers the team checks repeatedly.

Before choosing a plan, write down the weekly questions. How many real opportunities entered the pipeline? How long have deals sat without action? Which stage loses the most? What is likely to close this month? Which salesperson needs help, and why?

Build those reports from the trial data. Open several underlying deals and confirm the totals. Change a stage and see when the dashboard updates. Export the data and calculate one result separately.

If Growth answers the real questions, there is no reason to buy Pro for prettier charts. If management needs custom measures, test them before assuming the higher plan can reproduce an existing report.

Limited reporting and customization appear in the public feedback. This does not make Salesflare a poor CRM. It tells a growing company to test the report it already depends on.

## Permissions can change the plan choice

Growth is aimed at a single user or small team with simple access needs. Pro adds user permissions.

Permissions decide who can see or change a contact, deal, message, field, or report. They matter when the database includes private conversations, different regions, contractors, or managers who should approve changes.

Create a normal salesperson, a manager, and a temporary user. Check what each person can search, export, edit, delete, and see in the timeline. Remove the temporary user and confirm that company history remains while access ends.

If the team needs those controls, compare against Pro from the start. Growth is not cheaper when it cannot meet the company's basic access rules.

## The phone should handle the small urgent jobs

The mobile app does not need to reproduce every report. It should handle the work that happens away from a desk.

After a test meeting, add a note and change the deal stage. Then assign a task, find the last email, call the contact, and hand the lead to somebody else. Turn on notifications, then reduce them until only useful alerts remain.

Repeat the test with poor reception. Check whether an unfinished note survives and whether the app makes duplicate changes when the connection returns.

A mobile CRM is useful when a two-minute update prevents a forgotten promise. If the salesperson waits until evening because the app is awkward, the automatic timeline will still have a hole.

## What the plans cost

Growth costs $29 per user per month when billed annually or $39 with monthly billing. It includes the core CRM, automatic data input, email and website tracking, the sidebar, and the phone app. You also get personal email campaigns and five lead credits each month.

A lead credit is used to find or add outside contact information through Salesflare's lead tools. Five is enough to test the feature, not run large prospecting work.

Pro costs $49 per user per month billed annually or $64 monthly. It adds multi-step email workflows, user permissions, custom dashboards, and 100 monthly lead credits.

Enterprise costs $99 per user per month billed annually or $124 monthly. It adds custom training, data migration, a dedicated account manager, and 250 monthly lead credits.

Extra credits can be purchased. Added users are billed for the remaining part of the billing period. Disabling a user creates credit for unused time, which is applied to future invoices.

Price the actual team, including managers, contractors, and anyone who only needs occasional access. Ask which actions require a paid seat. Test how exports, stored history, workflows, and account ownership behave when a user is disabled.

## Export before moving the full customer list

Importing a CRM is easy to start and annoying to reverse.

Export the 100-record test. Check contacts, companies, custom fields, deal stages, owners, tasks, notes, dates, email references, attachments, and consent records. Open the files without Salesflare.

Some connected email content may remain in the original mailbox rather than the export. That can be reasonable, but the team needs to know what it can recover if it leaves.

Also test deletion and correction. Remove a test contact from Salesflare and check what remains in connected services, backups, and reports. Review the current privacy terms before connecting a real mailbox.

## Who should choose Salesflare?

Salesflare makes sense for a small B2B team that works mainly through email and regularly fails to maintain a traditional CRM. Its best feature is not a novel dashboard. It is the chance to create a useful customer history with less typing.

It is probably unnecessary for one person with a short, stable lead list. It is a poor match for a company that needs deep changes, complex permissions, or unusual sales routes. We would skip it if the trial cannot reproduce the reports managers need.

We did not connect an inbox, import contacts, or run an email sequence. We also did not test the phone, build a dashboard, export data, change a plan, or contact support. Ease of use, automatic capture, and responsive support have solid review-platform backing. The community sample is small.

Our rule is to import 100 representative records, then follow one real lead from first email to meeting, proposal, silence, and handoff. Compare every captured activity with the source. Look for missing events, wrong matches, and duplicates.

Build the weekly report, test the odd deal, use the phone after a meeting, check permissions, and export the result.

Keep Salesflare if the next person can open a lead and understand what happened and what to do. If the team keeps a private spreadsheet or asks for a recap anyway, the CRM has not solved the problem.
