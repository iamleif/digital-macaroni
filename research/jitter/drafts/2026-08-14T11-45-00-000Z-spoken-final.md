# Jitter review: plain-language final

Jitter is for making a static design move. You can start with text, a logo, a chart, or an app screen. Add movement in the browser, then save it as a video, GIF, or small animation for a website.

The most useful part is its connection with Figma. If the design already exists there, Jitter can bring over the separate layers. You do not have to rebuild the whole thing in a video editor. You can then make words appear, move parts of an interface, or animate a logo with presets and a simple timeline.

This does not make Jitter a replacement for After Effects. It gives a designer a faster way to handle the smaller jobs that often do not need After Effects in the first place.

## It works best when the design already exists

Imagine a software company has a Figma design of its new dashboard. It wants a short clip where the menu opens, a chart rises, and a button changes color.

The designer can import those layers into Jitter, add the movement, and export the clip. That is quicker than rebuilding the screen from flat screenshots. It also keeps the text, shapes, and other parts editable.

Users on Product Hunt and Reddit often praise this handoff. They also like the browser, templates, presets, and familiar design controls. Product Hunt currently shows 4.9 from 48 reviews. The samples elsewhere are much smaller.

The benefit is less obvious if the work does not begin in Figma. Jitter can still build designs and import other media, but the clean handoff is what makes it different from a normal editor.

## The free plan is a proper test

Free includes the main animation tools, more than 300 templates, Figma import, collaboration, and video, GIF, and Lottie export. Lottie is a small animation file often used inside websites and apps.

There are two limits that matter. A free workspace can hold three files, and exports stop at 720p and 30 frames per second. The finished file also carries a Jitter watermark.

That is enough to learn the controls and build a complete example. It is usually not enough for paid client work because the watermark identifies the tool on the final file.

Pro removes the watermark and allows unlimited files and folders. It raises output to 1080p at 60 frames per second. It also adds ProRes and WebM formats. Max is for people who need 4K or very high frame rates. It also adds a clear background and the option to save every frame separately. Ultra mainly increases the amount of AI use.

The official page offers monthly and annual billing per editor, with a 15% annual saving. Its numeric prices did not appear in the page response we could verify, so check the live total before paying.

## Easy controls do not create good motion

Jitter can make an object slide onto the screen in a few clicks. It cannot decide whether that movement helps the viewer understand anything.

In one Motion Design community thread, people looked at a marketing video made with Jitter. They focused on pacing, sound, and editing choices. That is the useful warning. A template can supply movement. The designer still decides when it starts, how long it lasts, and whether the scene has too much going on.

Jitter is less suited to complex effects, long video edits, or detailed character work. It also falls short when a project needs deep control over each part. A professional may use it for a quick product screen. They may still return to After Effects for harder work.

## Build one real project before upgrading

Do not test Jitter by dragging random templates onto a blank page. Bring in a design you already need to deliver. Animate the important parts, export it, and show it to someone without explaining what should happen.

If the viewer understands the message and the process felt quicker, the tool has done its job. Upgrade when the watermark, 720p limit, or three-file workspace stops that project from being delivered.

Do not pay for Max because 4K and 120 frames per second sound professional. Pay for it when a client, editor, or publishing system actually requires that output.

## Who should use Jitter

We recommend Jitter for designers and small marketing teams that already work in Figma and need short product clips, logo movement, social graphics, or simple web animations. It is useful because it removes a heavy setup from a small job.

We do not recommend it as a shortcut to becoming a motion designer. The controls are approachable, but the result still depends on the design, timing, sound, and restraint of the person using them.

Jitter gets the technical work out of the way. You still have to decide what should move and why.
