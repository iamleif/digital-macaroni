# Planable review: stop approving posts in screenshots

Planable is easiest to understand through the problem it removes. A social post is drafted in a spreadsheet. The client circles a screenshot in email. Somebody changes the caption in chat. Another person approves an older version, and nobody is sure which file should publish on Tuesday.

Planable puts the post, preview, comments, versions, approval, and publishing date together. That is its best job. It is not automatically the best tool for listening to every brand mention or producing the deepest social report.

We would buy it only if feedback stops arriving through email and chat. A polished approval screen does not help when the real decision still happens somewhere else.

## What a workspace does

Planable normally gives each client, brand, or location its own workspace. That space holds the connected social pages, content calendar, campaigns, drafts, people, and approval rules.

A writer can create versions for several networks and see previews that look close to the final posts. The internal team can comment before the client sees the work. A client can suggest a change or approve the post without reading a large spreadsheet.

Basic supports no approval or optional approval. Pro adds required approval, which prevents publication until somebody accepts the post. Enterprise can move a post through several ordered stages, such as writer, marketing manager, client, and legal.

The feed, calendar, grid, and list views help different people review the same work. The client may care about how an Instagram grid looks. The publisher may care about next week's calendar. An account manager may need a list of every post waiting for approval.

This review route is the strongest theme across a very large public sample. People repeatedly like the previews, familiar interface, central comments, calendar, and clear status. Agencies like that clients can use it without learning a complicated production system.

## Test it with the least technical approver

Do not let the person who set up Planable perform the whole trial. Invite the client or stakeholder who normally replies “looks good” to the wrong email thread.

Give that person a week of real content. Include a short text post, several images, a video, a story or short-form video where supported, a link, tags, a location, and a first comment. Use every social network and format that matters to the account.

Ask the client to change one sentence, reject one image, and approve another post. After approval, make a legitimate last-minute change. The system should make it obvious that the approved version changed and needs another decision.

Test private notes too. Internal comments about price, legal risk, or client confusion must not appear to the client. Invite a user with the wrong role on purpose and check what that person can see and publish.

At the end, ask the client three questions. Which posts are waiting on you? Which version will publish? What changed since your last review? If the answers are not obvious, the approval route still needs work.

Then count every email, message, screenshot, and document comment used during the week. The count should fall close to zero. Planable cannot centralize feedback that the team keeps accepting elsewhere.

## The live network post is the final test

Planable can show a convincing preview, but Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok, X, YouTube, Pinterest, Threads, and Google Business Profile control their own publishing rules. Their APIs change. Some formats, tags, music, collaboration options, or account types may require a notification and a final step inside the network app.

Publish a test for every important format. Compare the live post with the approved preview. Check text breaks, image crops, video cover, tags, links, first comment, location, thumbnail, and publish time.

Disconnect one social account before the trial ends. Tokens expire and network passwords change in real life. Make sure Planable tells the right person before the slot passes. Reconnect it and confirm which queued posts still need action.

Try a schedule change close to publication. Move a post across a daylight-saving change if the team works across time zones. Cancel one post and replace another. Make sure the old version does not publish after the new one.

Scheduling trouble, disconnections, and network-specific limits appear inside otherwise positive reviews. They are not reasons to reject Planable. They are reasons to test the exact formats instead of buying from the channel logos.

Keep a short checklist for formats that still require work in the native app. The approval record should state which parts the client actually approved.

## It is an approval product before it is a full social suite

Planable now includes optional analytics and a social inbox. The inbox brings comments and direct messages into one place. Analytics covers page and post results, audience information, reports, and suggested posting times.

Those additions may be enough for a team that mainly needs to publish and answer ordinary comments. They do not make Planable a complete social listening or customer-care platform.

Listening means finding public mentions even when the account was not tagged. Large inbox tools may have routing, service targets, detailed histories, and quality checks for many agents. Deeper analytics tools may compare competitors, campaigns, paid and organic work, and custom business results.

Recent comparisons draw the same boundary. Planable is especially polished for planning and approval. Tools built as broad social suites can go further on listening, inbox work, and reporting.

During the trial, route a busy day of real comments and messages through the inbox. Assign replies, leave one unanswered, hide or delete a comment where allowed, and test an angry message. Compare the result with the native networks.

Build the monthly report the client already receives. Do not accept a pretty default report if the contract requires different numbers. If the team still needs another inbox and reporting service, include both in the cost.

## Pricing grows by client, not by user

The first 50 posts are free. There is no time limit and no card is required. This is enough to test a small approval route.

Basic costs $33 per workspace per month. It includes 60 posts per month, four connected social pages, unlimited users, feed and calendar views, three campaigns, 10 GB of media storage, and no or optional approval.

Pro costs $49 per workspace per month. It includes 150 posts, ten pages, required approval, feed, calendar and grid views, ten campaigns, 50 GB of storage, a public API, and more version history.

Enterprise has custom pricing. It adds unlimited posts and campaigns, multi-level approval, all four views, SSO, a dedicated account manager, and bank payment.

Analytics is an add-on. The live page displays $14 per workspace at the monthly rate and $12 at the annual rate. The social inbox displays $9 monthly and $7.50 at the annual rate. Annual main plans give two months free. Prices exclude tax.

A workspace is not a user seat. Planable recommends one workspace for each client, brand, or separate location. Five clients normally mean five subscriptions. Unlimited users help because the agency does not pay again for every client reviewer, but they do not reduce the workspace bill.

Price the actual agency. List every client, brand, and location. Add the needed main plan, analytics, inbox, and likely growth in posts or pages.

Five Pro workspaces cost $245 per month before add-ons and tax. Add monthly analytics and inbox to each one, and the total becomes $360. That may be excellent value if it removes hours of chasing approval. It may be too high for a client posting four times a month.

Decide whether the agency absorbs the cost or passes it through. Test what happens when a client leaves. Export the content, comments, approvals, reports, and media that must remain. Published posts have limited storage periods, so Planable should not be the only permanent archive.

## The large review sample is useful

G2 showed 1,033 reviews and Capterra showed 350. Planable has much more customer evidence than many products in this review batch. The repeated approval and ease themes deserve real weight.

G2 and Capterra share ownership in 2026, so they are not two fully independent markets. Review invitations and the high share of agencies and small companies also shape the pool. TrustRadius and community discussions provide smaller counter-samples.

The complaint themes are consistent enough to plan around: cost, plan limits, publishing trouble, network restrictions, and lighter suite depth. We did not create a workspace, invite a client, publish a post, or contact support.

## Who should choose Planable?

Planable is a strong choice to test for an agency or marketing team whose real problem is client review. It suits work where several people must see the post, leave a clear correction, and record approval before publishing.

It is unnecessary for one person who writes and publishes without review. It may also be the wrong main product when listening, high-volume customer care, or advanced reporting matters more than approval.

Our rule is to run one real campaign from draft to live post. Invite the least technical approver. Make that person reject, change, and approve content. Change one approved post. Test private notes and the wrong permission.

Publish every required format, disconnect one account, and compare the live result with the approved preview. Build the real report and handle a busy inbox day.

Count every correction that arrives outside Planable. Then price all clients and add-ons. Keep Planable when the outside feedback disappears and the published version matches the decision the client made. If people keep approving screenshots in chat, the workflow has not changed.
