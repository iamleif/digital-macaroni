folk is a CRM for small teams that want something lighter than traditional sales software. It keeps people, companies, emails, meetings, notes, pipeline stages, and follow-up work in one shared place.

The product feels closer to a smart contact list than a system built for a sales department with layers of administration. That is its main appeal. A partnership manager, agency owner, recruiter, or small sales team can see who a person is. They can also see what happened last and what should happen next.

Our view is simple. folk is useful when the team needs a shared memory and one clear next action for each relationship. It is a harder choice when management needs deep forecasts, strict permissions, complex approvals, or many specialized sales reports.

We recommend folk for a small team that wants a flexible contact list without the weight of a large sales system.

## What folk helps a small team do

A spreadsheet can store names and stages. It does not naturally capture the email thread, last meeting, source of the relationship, and next reminder. Those details tend to live in separate inboxes and in people's heads.

folk connects email, calendar, and WhatsApp accounts so the relationship record can show more of that history. A browser extension can capture someone from LinkedIn or another page. Contact enrichment can fill missing public details such as a work email, company, or job title.

The team can place people into groups and show a group as a pipeline. A pipeline is a set of stages, such as New lead, Contacted, Meeting booked, Proposal, and Won. Each person moves across the stages as the relationship changes.

This is not only for direct sales. An investor can track founders. A recruiter can track candidates. An agency can track clients and partners. The common need is knowing who owns the relationship and what happens next.

The large review sample repeatedly praises folk's simple setup, modern interface, relationship history, enrichment, and browser capture. Those are practical strengths for a team leaving a spreadsheet or rejecting a heavier CRM.

## Automatic contact data needs checking

Capturing and enriching a contact saves research, but it does not make the information correct.

Two people may share a name. Someone may have changed jobs. A browser capture and an email sync may create separate records for the same person. A general company address may be mistaken for a direct work email.

Import a dirty sample during the trial. Use 100 contacts with duplicates, missing companies, old jobs, and more than one email address. Let folk merge what it can. Then inspect every suggested match and find the history that did not land where expected.

Also decide which source wins when data conflicts. If a person changes a title in a form but enrichment later finds an older one, which should remain? A CRM becomes less useful when people stop trusting the details.

The browser extension deserves the same test. Capture a person who already exists, a person with two roles, and someone whose public page has limited information. Check whether folk updates, duplicates, or asks before changing the record.

This work is not unique to folk. Every CRM import has cleanup. The point is to measure it before the full address book moves.

## Email history creates a privacy question

A shared relationship history is useful because another teammate can understand what happened. It can also expose a conversation that was not meant for the whole workspace.

folk's Standard plan shares the last 30 interactions, while Premium includes the full history. Enterprise adds controls for internal and sensitive interactions and permissions at the group level.

Before connecting every inbox, use two test accounts. Include a normal client thread, an internal discussion, and a sensitive message. Check what another member can see. Then remove access and confirm what remains.

The same caution applies to outgoing email. Standard supports campaigns and up to 2,000 messages per member each month. Premium raises this to 5,000 and adds sequences. A sequence is a timed set of follow-up emails that changes or stops after a reply.

Send a small campaign to addresses you control. Test a reply, an unsubscribe, a bounce, and a person who already answered through another channel. Check that the sequence stops when it should. Email limits do not guarantee good delivery, so use a real sending domain and watch the results before scaling.

## The mobile complaint has changed

Older folk feedback often mentioned the lack of a mobile app. That is no longer a fair current complaint. folk now lists a mobile app across its plans.

The current question is whether the app supports the work someone needs after a meeting. Can a person find the contact, add a note, change the stage, and set a follow-up while walking out of the room? We did not test that flow, so we cannot judge its quality from the fact that the app exists.

This is an important research rule. Old customer feedback can explain the product's history, but it should not be repeated after the product changes.

## folk pricing

folk gives every new workspace a 14-day trial with Premium features and does not require a card. At the end, the workspace is blocked until the team upgrades or receives an extension. The data is not deleted at once.

Standard costs $24 per member each month when billed as $288 for a year. Month-to-month billing costs $30. It includes pipelines, email campaigns, enrichment, AI assistants and fields, the browser extension, account sync, and the wider integration catalog.

Premium costs $48 per member monthly with annual billing, or $60 month to month. It adds custom record types and deals, email sequences, workspace roles, dashboards, API access, more credits, and full interaction history.

Enterprise starts at $80 per member monthly with annual billing, or $100 month to month. It adds custom limits, deeper controls for private interactions, group permissions, guided setup, custom integrations, and priority support.

Usage limits matter alongside seats. Standard includes 500 email-finding credits, 2,000 AI-field credits, and 2,000 outgoing messages per member each month. Premium increases those amounts and supports more synced accounts.

A five-person team on annual Standard pays $1,440 for a year. On Premium it pays $2,880. The difference should be justified by a real need for dashboards, sequences, roles, API access, full history, or custom record types.

## Who should choose folk?

folk suits a small team that wins business through personal relationships and wants more structure than a spreadsheet. It is particularly appealing when people avoid a traditional CRM because it feels like paperwork.

It is less suitable when the company needs complex forecasting, quotes, territories, approval chains, detailed activity controls, or many specialist reports. Premium has dashboards and custom deals, but those features should be tested against the exact weekly sales meeting, not assumed from their names.

Our decision rule is practical. Import 100 messy contacts. Test merge and enrichment. Connect two accounts with sensitive threads. Send a small campaign with replies and bounces. Use the mobile app after a real meeting. Finally, rebuild the weekly management report.

Choose folk if normal teammates keep the contacts current and every relationship ends with a clear next step. Look elsewhere if managers need to rebuild the numbers outside folk. Do the same if the team spends more time fixing contact history than using it.

folk is a lighter CRM, not a small version of every enterprise sales system. For the right small team, that focus is the reason to buy it. The real test is whether that simplicity survives your data, privacy needs, and reports. A clean demo is not enough.
