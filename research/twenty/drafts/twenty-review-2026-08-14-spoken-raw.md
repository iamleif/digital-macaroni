## The short answer

Twenty is a promising open CRM. It has the cleanest product story in this part of the market: modern screens, a data model you can change, and code your team can inspect.

Our problem with it is simple. Twenty asks you to judge two very different products under one name. The managed cloud app looks like a straightforward CRM. The self-hosted version makes your team responsible for upgrades, backups, and any bad data move.

That divide controls the verdict. Twenty is a good fit for a technical team that wants more control than a standard cloud CRM allows. It is a poor fit for anyone who wants a finished sales system with deep reports, mobile work, and low-risk upkeep.

## What it is good at

Twenty gets the basic feel right. Its clean screens and simple flow are real strengths. A CRM holds a lot of dull data, so a clear screen matters more than it sounds.

The data model is also easy to shape. Teams can create custom objects and fields for work that does not fit a stock sales process. The API gives a tech team more ways to move data in and out.

Open code is another draw. Buyers can inspect the project, host it, and build changes that a closed CRM may never add. The GitHub project had about 47,200 stars and 6,700 forks when we did this research. It also had more than 12,000 commits.

Those figures show strong interest and active work. They do not prove that every user is happy or that each release is safe. They do show that Twenty is not an abandoned side project.

## Where it gets in the way

Self-hosting is where Twenty stops being a simple CRM choice. You avoid giving full control to a vendor, but your team takes on setup, backups, updates, and repair work.

Upgrades are the part we would watch. Small installs can move through an update without much trouble. Failed upgrades can also require hands-on work in the database. Both outcomes are possible because the data size, old version, and server setup may differ.

Twenty also has less depth than older CRM tools. Marketing tools, mobile use, reports, app links, access rules, and complex flows all need checking. These notes can age fast because the project ships often. Still, no buyer should assume those basics are there without checking the current release.

Support is harder to judge. Slow replies appear in the sample, but not often enough to call them a common fault. It is enough to rule out a top support score.

## What using it is likely to feel like

This is where the review splits in two. The cloud product earns praise for its clean screens and flexible records. For a small team that needs to track people, deals, and a few custom fields, Twenty looks much lighter than an old-school CRM.

The self-hosted product carries a different burden. The public accounts do not show a small annoyance. They show a real risk: an upgrade can go smoothly on a small setup, or it can fail and send someone into database repair.

That makes Twenty a product to qualify, not one to buy on appearance. A self-hosted team should first test a backup, an upgrade, and a restore with fake records. If those three steps are not routine, the control Twenty offers is not worth the cost.

The cloud plan avoids most of that work. It does not solve the other question, though: whether Twenty has enough CRM depth for your sales process. A clean screen cannot replace reporting, access rules, mobile work, or marketing tools that a team already depends on.

## Price and value

Twenty offers a managed cloud service and open code for self-hosting. We are not listing a price because the stored research does not prove a current plan figure.

Self-hosting is not free in practice. It needs a server, backups, updates, and a person who can fix a failed move. Those costs may still be fair for a team that wants control over its data and code.

The cloud plan is easier to judge. Compare its cost with the time saved by the clean screens and custom data model. If you still need several outside tools to cover the CRM gaps, the value drops fast.

## Who it is for

Twenty fits a small technical team that wants a CRM it can shape. It also makes sense for a firm that prefers open code and is willing to own more of the setup.

Developers may like the API and the chance to build their own links. A team with an unusual data model may also get more room than a fixed sales tool gives them.

Skip it if you need deep marketing tools, rich mobile work, or complex reports today. It is also a poor fit for a team with no one to care for a self-hosted app.

Large firms with strict access needs should check every rule in the current release. Do not assume a popular GitHub project has the same controls as an old business CRM.

## Verdict

Twenty is one of the more appealing open CRM tools. It has a clear product direction, an active project, and more room to shape data than a normal cloud CRM.

It is not the safe choice for every buyer. The feature gaps matter. Self-hosted upgrades can demand real skill. Those are not minor tradeoffs.

Choose the cloud plan if you want the design without server work. Choose self-hosting only after it passes a backup and upgrade test. Older CRM tools still win when depth and low-risk upkeep matter more than control.
