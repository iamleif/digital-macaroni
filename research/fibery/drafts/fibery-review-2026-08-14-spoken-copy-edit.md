Fibery is a work platform that lets a company design its own system. You can create lists for customers, projects, tasks, research, goals, or almost anything else. Then you link the records so the work does not sit in separate boxes.

For example, one customer interview can link to the customer, three requested features, the product project that addresses them, and the final release note. A person can start from any one of those records and follow the rest of the story.

This is much more flexible than a normal project board. It is also much more work to set up. Fibery is a good choice when those links remove a real handoff between teams. It is a poor choice when all you need is a list of tasks and due dates.

We recommend Fibery for teams with unusual workflows and enough time to shape the system around them.

## What makes Fibery different

Fibery calls each list a database. A database might hold Customers, Projects, Tasks, Interviews, or Invoices. One item in the list is an entity. A relation is the direct link between two items, such as a task linked to its project and the customer paying for it.

Those plain links are the heart of the product. A product team can connect interview notes to feature ideas. An agency can connect a client to projects, work, meeting notes, and invoices. An operations team can connect a goal to the projects and measures that support it.

The same information can appear in several views. Tasks may appear as a table, board, calendar, or timeline. Documents and whiteboards can sit beside structured records. Forms collect new items. Automations change fields or send data when something happens.

This can replace parts of a project tool, wiki, research store, and simple CRM. The review pools consistently praise that freedom and the ability to keep related knowledge together. Support and the pace of product updates are also common strengths.

The benefit only appears when a link solves a problem. Connecting every list to every other list because Fibery allows it will create a maze. Start with the facts people copy between tools today. Build only the links that stop that copying or help someone make a decision.

## The blank workspace is the real cost

Fibery does not know how your company should describe its work. That freedom is useful, but it means someone has to decide.

Should a product request and a feature be separate records? Can one task belong to several projects? Who can see customer notes? What happens when a project is complete? Which field is required? Which view should a new teammate open first?

These are process questions, not software settings. A template can give the team a starting point, but it cannot settle them. This explains the strongest complaint in the customer feedback: Fibery takes time to learn and set up.

The risk is not only a slow launch. One enthusiastic person can build a clever workspace that nobody else understands. If every small change requires that person, the team has created a new internal software dependency.

Name a workspace owner, but do not let that person design alone. Put two normal users in the trial. Ask them to create, find, update, and finish real work without a guided tour. Watch the names they misunderstand and the views they avoid. A simpler model that people use is better than a perfect model that lives in one person's head.

You also need rules for change. Decide who can add databases and fields, how old views are removed, and where the design is documented. Fibery can adapt as the business changes, but constant unplanned changes can make it harder to trust.

## Test the weak spots, not the easy board

Most flexible work tools can make a pleasant task board. That is not a useful trial for Fibery.

Build the report that management actually asks for. If the business plans staff across several projects, try the real resource view. If clients need access, invite one as a guest and confirm exactly what they can see. If customer data is private, build the hardest permission case and test it with separate accounts.

Reporting, external sharing, permission clarity, notifications, and detailed resource planning appear as narrower concerns in the customer feedback. They are not reasons to reject Fibery. They are reasons to put those tasks at the front of the trial.

Test export as well. Fibery can export a workspace to Markdown and CSV. Download it and inspect whether the records and links would be usable outside the product. An export button is not the same as an easy move, but it is better to learn the gap before the workspace holds years of company history.

We did not build a workspace, run an import, or test current mobile use. Some older community comments describe product gaps that may no longer exist, so we have not repeated them as current facts. The verdict here rests on the current product and repeated patterns in larger review pools, not on a hands-on result.

## Fibery pricing

The Free plan supports up to ten users and ten databases. It allows unlimited records, basic views, documents, formulas, automations, and one synced integration. It includes 14 days of version history and lower monthly limits for automation and data sync.

Standard costs €12 per paid user each month with annual billing. It removes the database cap and adds charts, whiteboards, human support, more history, higher limits, and AI credits.

Pro costs €20 per paid user with annual billing. It adds custom apps, stronger permissions, scripted automations, unlimited version history, more AI, and much higher automation and sync limits. Enterprise costs €40 per paid user, requires at least 25 paid users, and adds identity controls, more data regions, unlimited automation and integrations, and a customer success manager.

Month-to-month prices are higher. The pricing page offers a twenty percent saving for annual billing. Every new workspace receives a 14-day Pro trial.

Observers and guests are free on paid plans. An observer can read and comment broadly. A guest works with selected records, which is useful for a client or contractor. Guest limits and the number of shared records vary by plan, so test the real client setup instead of assuming free access covers it.

The plan table also includes limits for automation runs, synced records, AI credits, history, and permission tools. Count those alongside seats. A low seat total can still need Pro because of one permission or automation requirement.

## Who should choose Fibery?

Fibery suits a team whose important work crosses the borders between a project tool, CRM, wiki, and research store. It is especially useful when the same customers, projects, and decisions keep being copied between those tools.

It is unnecessary for a team with a standard process that already fits a focused product. A normal task manager will be faster to adopt. A dedicated CRM will often have deeper sales features. A dedicated resource planner may produce the exact capacity report an agency needs.

Our decision rule is simple. Pick one workflow that crosses two teams. Build it with real records, one automation, the hardest management report, and the hardest permission case. Run it for two weeks. Ask whether the links removed copying and whether normal users can work without the designer beside them.

Do not move the whole company during the trial. Fibery deserves the setup work only after one connected workflow proves the point. When that proof exists, the flexibility can be genuinely useful. Without it, the team is paying to design a system it may never need.
