# Digital Macaroni review brief

Use this brief before researching, drafting, editing, or publishing any review.

Our job is simple: help a reader decide whether a piece of software is worth their time and money. We use the product story, public facts, and many real user signals to form a clear point of view. We do not act like the product's marketing team, and we do not pretend a research review is hands-on testing.

## Before you write

- Confirm the product is in scope: useful, established enough to research, and not a household-name default unless there is a strong reason to cover it.
- Verify the product's original public release date from a defensible dated source. Keep this date private. Never show it in the article, structured data, feed, or page copy.
- Collect at least six sources: the official site, two independent review platforms, and at least one real community discussion. Look for at least 20 user voices in total.
- Read for agreement and disagreement. Do not turn one loud complaint or one glowing review into the whole verdict.
- Save sources, key facts, unknowns, and score reasoning in the product evidence record before drafting.
- Do not publish if the release date, evidence, or major claims cannot be supported.

## Writing rules

- Write like a smart human talking to another human. Be plain, specific, and useful.
- Write for natural speech first. Use a sixth- or seventh-grade readability score as a final warning, not as a drafting formula. Mix short judgments with longer explanations when the thought needs them.
- Do not use em dashes or en dashes.
- YOU MUST TAKE A STAND And give a definitive answer for the review. Real editorial judgment.
- Do not hedge at all and do not use too many adjectives. 
- Nothing should come in threes, they should come in twos or fours. 
- Do not use any stupid phrases like and here's the kicker, Or here's what most people missed. 
- Do not use any filler words. 
- Do not use its not x its y... and so on. 
- Do not copy source wording. Summarize patterns in your own words.
- Never claim we tested a product unless we actually did.
- Do not make up features, prices, customer stories, or certainty.
- A research-based piece must still read as a real review. Write our judgment from the evidence, not a feature summary followed by a caveat.
- Treat the evidence as material the publication has already digested. The article must speak from Digital Macaroni's editorial viewpoint, not narrate the research corpus.
- Own the conclusion. Write "Lodgify's channel sync is the main risk," not "reports describe channel-sync failures."
- Do not use source-counting or aggregation phrases such as "reviews show," "the evidence shows," "users report," "sources describe," or "the complaints are not dominant." Keep source attribution in metadata unless a narrow qualifier is essential to avoid overstating a reported pattern.
- Use the publication's "we" only for a genuine opinion or recommendation. Do not add it merely to make a sentence sound authoritative.
- Start with a clear answer. Explain the main reason to buy and the concern that could change the decision.
- Every section must answer one of these: what is our judgment, why does it matter to the buyer, or who should act on it. Cut anything that does none of those jobs.
- Do not use empty experience language such as "should feel," "likely to feel," "can be a good fit," or generic steps that could describe any product.
- Keep research-method disclosure in the review metadata. Mention an evidence limit in the article only when that limit changes the recommendation.
- Explain what the product is, who it helps, where it falls short, and who should skip it.
- Keep the card verdict to eight or nine words. It must be clear at a glance.
- Explain unfamiliar product terms through what actually happens. Write "a booking on Airbnb should block the same dates on Vrbo" before using "calendar sync." Skip the jargon if the reader does not need it.
- Keep private planning labels out of the article. Do not write "buyer fit," "decision rule," "value proposition," "the case is weaker," "earns its place," "operational complexity," or similar analyst language.
- Explain each important feature in normal terms: what it lets someone do, who needs it, whether it works well enough, its main limit, and when it will not matter.
- Use headings that a reader can scan. Avoid filler, buzzwords, and generic advice.

## Spoken review brief

Before drafting, answer these in ordinary spoken language:

- What is the product?
- Who will find it useful?
- What does its main feature actually let them do?
- Is that feature good, good enough, limited, annoying, or unreliable?
- When will the feature not matter?
- What happened to real customers that changed our opinion?
- What should a friend check before paying?

Draft from this brief, not directly from the source packet or private argument card.

## Loose article outline

Use only the sections the review needs. Do not force all seven or organize the body around the company's plan names.

### 1. The short answer

Open with two or three short paragraphs. Say what the product does, the main reason someone would choose it, and the central tradeoff. Give the reader a real answer early.

### 2. What it is good at

Explain the strongest part of the product with concrete examples. Tie this to repeated user feedback and official product facts. Include one short callout with the clearest takeaway.

### 3. Where it gets in the way

Cover the meaningful limits, not minor nitpicks. This may be price, setup, missing features, support, reliability, complexity, or a poor fit for a certain kind of buyer. Be fair and specific.

### 4. What using it is like

Include this only when hands-on notes or detailed user accounts support it. Describe the actual steps. Do not guess how the product feels.

### 5. Price and value

State current pricing only when it is confirmed from the official source. Explain who gets value at that price and who probably does not. Do not turn this into a pricing table unless the article truly needs one.

### 6. Who it is for

Name the people, teams, and use cases that fit the product. Then name who should look elsewhere. Clear exclusions make the recommendation more useful.

### 7. Verdict

End with a direct judgment. Restate the best fit, the biggest caution, and whether the product is worth trying. The conclusion should match the score and the article, with no new claims.

## Score check

Score the whole experience out of 10. Use these four lenses:

- Onboarding: Can a new person get useful work done without friction?
- Product: Does it solve its main job well and reliably?
- Support: Can a customer get useful help when something goes wrong?
- Billing: Is the price clear and fair for the value?

The overall score is an editorial judgment, not a mathematical average. It must be explainable from the evidence and must not conflict with the written verdict.

## Final review before publish

- Does the opening give a real opinion, not a feature list?
- Are all factual claims supported in the evidence record?
- Does the piece include both strengths and limits?
- Does it sound like a person, not a press release or an AI summary?
- Is the language simple, with no em dashes or en dashes, without becoming clipped?
- Does every unfamiliar term get translated into something the product actually does?
- Could a real person say each paragraph aloud without sounding like an analyst or marketer?
- Does the verdict, score, and card copy say the same thing?
- Is the release-date record private and the publication date after it?
- Has the content validator passed?

If any answer is no, revise or hold the article. Do not publish it yet.
